Five minutes and counting

Last week adidas launched a 5-minute ad for the World Cup. Five minutes. I've been thinking about that a lot.

Professor Karen Nelson-Field's attention research shows that 85% of digital ads globally get less than 2.5 seconds of attention. So a brand releasing a five-minute film feels either very brave or very wrong.

When ads don't meet the Attention-Memory Threshold
of 2.5 seconds, it's hard for memory to grow.

© Amplified Intelligence

But watch it and you start to understand why it might just work.

It's adidas — one of the most recognised sports brands on the planet. It's football, which is peak interest for a huge chunk of the global population. It features Hollywood star power alongside the biggest names in the game, past and present. And it's built around the World Cup — the single biggest moment in the football calendar.

That's not one ingredient. That's the full recipe: decades of brand equity, genuine cultural relevance in football, the biggest stars money can buy, a production budget to match, and the global media budget to support it across every ad length. I spotted a shorter cut playing on Sky during the weekend football — it's likely a lot of the media spend is going against the 30 or 60-second edits.

The attention research I've been exploring shows that passive, low-interest content gets brutally short viewing times. But high-interest content — content that people genuinely want to watch — operates by completely different rules. adidas has essentially created a piece of entertainment that happens to be an ad.

Could any other brand pull this off? Very few could.

That's the lesson worth taking from this. It's not "five-minute ads are back." It's understanding why this one has a fighting chance of being watched — and being honest that most brands don't have those ingredients.

Know what you're working with before you decide how long to talk.
