The Greatest Show

Paul Feldwick's The Anatomy of Humbug changed how I think about advertising. Not as communication, not as persuasion — but as showmanship. Feldwick traces a lineage from PT Barnum through to modern brand advertising, and the argument is simple: people don't engage with ads because they're informative. They engage because they're entertaining. Because someone put on a show.

The Greatest Showman understood this before the industry had a framework for it.

At The National Lottery I found this in our Dream Inspector character for the EuroMillions campaign. His performance elevated the ad. Any social content or events became much more theatrical from his showmanship.

This summer is a World Cup summer. From my time at adidas I know what that means — months of build, pressure, creative ambition, a global stage. So I was watching closely when Backyard Legends dropped.

What struck me most was Timothée Chalamet. Not the players, not the tricks — him. He led the narrative. He was the showman. That casting decision unlocked everything else: the energy, the nostalgia, the blend of world-class footballers and street football culture. It felt like a performance.

I love the ad. To my former colleagues heading into one of the most exciting summers in sport — over to you.
